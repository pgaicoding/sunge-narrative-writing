# Narrative Mechanics

Use this reference when a draft needs structural design, scene repair, or a stronger middle. These are transferable mechanisms, not a style-imitation recipe.

## 1. Conflict and the Hidden Question

Write the story contradiction in one line:

```text
The viewpoint can control ___, but cannot secure ___, so every attempt raises ___ instead.
```

Then choose one hidden question the reader will carry. Every important scene should intensify, complicate, or partially answer it. A second question may support it; a competing central question splits attention.

## 2. Opening: Conflict Before Context

Open on a specific imbalance the reader can picture:

```text
large scale vs. small scale
small action vs. irreversible consequence
familiar routine vs. one broken step
direct request vs. delayed answer
```

The opening owes the reader a concrete object or action, active pressure, and a question that can survive several scenes. It does not owe a biography.

**Diagnostic:** If the first 150 Chinese characters can move to paragraph five without loss, the opening probably contains context rather than pressure.

## 3. Seven-Beat Path for Long Emotional Arcs

Use this only when the material benefits from a long tightening arc:

1. **crisis cold open:** show the rupture without explaining it;
2. **distant cause:** jump back to the earliest desire or rule;
3. **encounter:** stage the moment the two forces meet;
4. **evidence of closeness:** make attachment visible through action, not a label;
5. **anomaly accumulation:** repeat the established rule under increasing pressure;
6. **rule break:** one consequential exception changes the reading of prior scenes;
7. **aftermath return:** let remaining work or an altered object close the action.

The beats are functions, not mandatory headings. Combine or replace them for profiles, case studies, and short essays.

## 4. Scene Evidence: Details With a Job

A useful detail performs at least two functions:

- makes the scene visible;
- reveals hierarchy, desire, fear, cost, or absence;
- creates a future return;
- controls tempo;
- contradicts what a character says.

Prefer a worker repeating a needless task, a device still sending an old alert, a receipt with one odd line, or a hand that stops midway over generic wealth adjectives or emotion labels. The point is not excess; it is pressure made physical.

Use the `actor + action + friction + implication` test. If the implication requires a full explanatory paragraph, choose a stronger action.

## 5. Motif Register

Choose ordinary evidence that can survive the whole piece. Give each motif three states:

| Stage | Function | Reader effect |
|---|---|---|
| appearance | practical or emotionally neutral | recognition |
| variation | handled, lost, repaired, refused, or misunderstood | re-interpretation |
| return | present under changed conditions | emotional remainder |

Short work usually needs one dominant and one supporting motif; never use more than four. Do not announce symbolism. Do not repeat identical wording. Meaning must change through events.

## 6. Repeat–Exception Engine

Repetition works only after the reader learns a rule.

```text
establish the response → repeat under changed conditions → vary the cost → break it once
```

The exception should alter a decision, relationship, or self-understanding. If nothing changes, it is merely emphasis. If the text breaks the rule repeatedly, no rule was established.

This engine can operate through a reply, a permission, a missed call, a workflow step, a form of address, or a routine action. Do not borrow a source's signature repeated word.

## 7. Setup–Payoff Ledger

For every seed, record the promised question, expected return window, and actual consequence.

Strong payoffs do one of four things:

- answer the literal question but deepen the emotional one;
- turn a tender detail into evidence of distance;
- expose the cost hidden inside an earlier convenience;
- make a repeated behavior impossible to continue.

A coincidence that solves the conflict is not a payoff. A return with no consequence is a callback, not a payoff.

## 8. Time and Distance

Time structure often tightens in three distances:

- **long view:** compress years or eras to establish the governing desire;
- **middle view:** stage several representative encounters and growing anomalies;
- **close view:** slow down around the exception, silence, and immediate aftermath.

Do not march through the calendar. Cut after a charged image, withheld answer, or decision. Bridge with the same object, question, routine, or consequence at two times. On the far side, enter at the first changed condition.

## 9. Dialogue, Viewpoint, and Address

Every important line should act: ask, price, test, stall, soothe, provoke, grant, or leave.

Build a dialogue beat with:

```text
line → silence or gesture → altered leverage
```

Let a small reply carry a large decision. Avoid reciprocal speeches of equal length. If both people state exactly what they feel, subtext has vanished.

Keep the viewpoint limited. Separate what is observed, remembered, attributed, and inferred. A shift from full name to nickname, role, pronoun, or no address can mark relational distance, but only if the pattern is established and the change is earned.

## 10. Rhythm and Emotional Restraint

Treat paragraph length as a speed control.

- **Compress:** travel, repeated successes, ordinary chronology, known background.
- **Expand:** a manual action, hesitation, boundary test, irreversible click, or room after someone leaves.
- **Withhold:** the answer the viewpoint fears to hear.

At the peak, move one step away from declared feeling. Show a body measurement that stays ordinary, a routine performed incorrectly, an unused object, cleanup, or a schedule that continues after the event ends. Constant staccato destroys contrast; vary the sentence field.

## 11. Consequence Inventory and Ending

After the turn, list what remains:

- unfinished labor;
- cleanup, repair, refund, or storage;
- unused supplies;
- reminders and schedules;
- objects whose operating method has been forgotten;
- rooms, routes, or systems still following the old plan.

Choose one item that transforms an earlier scene. Draft three endings: object return, altered routine, and spatial or temporal echo. Close the action while leaving the human question slightly open. Remove the moral if the evidence already carries it.

## 12. Causal Check

Reduce every scene to one line:

```text
Because scene A changes ___, scene B becomes necessary.
```

If scenes connect only with “and then,” reorder, merge, or cut. Narrative drive comes from consequence, not chronology alone.
